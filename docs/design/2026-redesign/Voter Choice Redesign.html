Voter Choice
Design proposal · v1 May 2026 For review

A redesign for Voter Choice, framed around one question: did your Congress earn its job back?

The original app tried to be a general ballot helper. With November 2026 in view — and the country's 250th — the most useful version of this tool is a tight, opinionated workflow that turns a chat conversation into a one‑page printable ballot. Below: what I'd change, why, and how the screens look.

01 What's not working today

Four things to fix before anything else.

None of these are about pixels — they're about what the tool is asking the user to do, and how clearly. The current build asks for trust before it has earned it, and asks for decisions before it has explained anything.

Identity drift

The homepage says "Congress," then changes the subject.

The hero is about Congress. Three scrolls down it's about polling places, key dates, what ID to bring, and uploading a profile. The promised story — here's how your reps actually voted — never gets told above the fold.

Fix: hero stays on one idea; civic logistics (where to vote, ID, dates) move into a small pre‑poll footer card that appears after a ballot is built.
No artifact in view

The chat is a chat. The ballot is invisible.

The whole point of the tool is the printable ballot, but during the conversation there's no sign of it filling in. Users can't tell how far they've gotten or what's left. Decisions feel disposable.

Fix: split workspace. Chat on the left, the actual ballot — the thing you'll print — on the right, updating as you decide.
Button soup

Below the chat there are too many equal‑weight buttons.

"Save profile," "Print ballot," "Handoff," "Restart," "What's on my ballot," "Tell me about my rep" — all competing for the same row. Nothing reads as primary.

Fix: only suggestions belong above the input. Exports (PDF, profile, handoff) live in the ballot pane where they make sense.
Data without weight

"Voting record" and "donors" are claims, not visualizations.

When the visualization fails (as the user noted), the chat collapses to long paragraphs of text — which is exactly the news‑article wall the tool was meant to replace.

Fix: inline candidate cards with alignment bars + a donor stacked bar. Robust enough that even a partial render still reads.
02 Design principles

Three rules I'd hold the redesign to.

P · 01

One job, named on every screen.

"Build a ballot you'll print." Every section, button and message answers to it. Anything that doesn't help the user finish a ballot gets pushed below the fold or cut.

P · 02

The record carries the page.

Numbers do the persuading, not adjectives. Vote alignment as bars. Donations as a stacked bar. Sources cited inline. No editorial verbs ("courageously voted"); show, don't tell.

P · 03

Treat the paper ballot as the real product.

The printable PDF is the deliverable. The web app is the wizard for filling it in. Design the paper first, then design the chat that produces it.

Addendum · Edge cases & the harder questions

Five states that need design love: the cold open, the moment Civic API misses, where polling logistics actually live, the day the free chat budget runs out, and a Polis‑style neighbors view that actually says something.

07 The cold open

Let them talk first. Mirror back the themes.

An earlier draft of this proposal opened with a 12‑chip issue picker. Wrong move — even a thoughtful list presupposes our taxonomy, and a non‑partisan tool shouldn't tell voters which boxes their concerns belong in. Real opening: ask an open question, let them write in their own words, then have the AI mirror back the themes it inferred — with the user's exact phrases quoted as evidence. The ranking emerges from their language, and they confirm or reorder before anything downstream uses it.

Voter Choice · AI

I've pulled your sample ballot for Harris County, TX‑7. Before I walk you through races, I want to know what you're judging candidates on — in your words, not from a pre‑built list.

What's been on your mind this year? Things you wish Congress would actually do something about. Frustrations, hopes, fights you've watched in your community. Type as much or as little as you want.

Why this opening

Their language, not our taxonomy.

"Insulin formulary changes" carries more signal than checking "Healthcare." Free‑form gives the AI specific lived experience to work with — and it lets the user raise things no pre‑built list would include ("the council ignored the flood report," "ICE detention near my kid's school").

The AI shows its work. Every inferred theme cites the exact phrase from the user's own message. You can see how it mapped your language — and correct it if it generalized badly.
Reordering is the confirmation. The drag‑to‑rank step is the moment of consent. Until the user locks the ranking, no candidate gets scored against anything. The AI's inference is a proposal, not a verdict.
"Give me a prompt" is for users who freeze at a blank text box. Sample prompts: "Tell me about a frustration you've absorbed silently this year." · "What conversation keeps coming up at your kitchen table?"
Three is fine. So is five. No artificial slot count. If the user only describes two things they care about, they get a two‑theme list. Density of conviction beats apparent completeness.
08 When the API doesn't have your ballot

Honesty about the data, with one clear path forward.

Google Civic doesn't cover most local races. Pretending otherwise loses trust. Tell the user, hand them their county's sample‑ballot link, and let them paste the contest list — with a PII warning loud enough that no one accidentally pastes their name and DOB.

Why three tabs

The fallback is the most fragile moment in the tool.

If a user can't proceed past local races, the printable ballot is incomplete and the trip to the polls didn't get easier. Three explicit paths beats one unclear textarea.

The PII warning is non‑optional. Red border, lives next to the textarea, not above it where it gets scrolled past. List is concrete — naming the exact fields someone might paste is far more effective than "don't paste personal info."
County link is offered, not required. A user who knows where their sample ballot is doesn't need to be tutored; one who doesn't gets a direct link to their county, not a generic "look it up" message.
"Skip local races" is a real option. A user who only cares about Congress can finish the federal section and print. Don't trap them.

12 Amending themes mid‑session

Themes are a living document, not a one‑time gate.

A user might be three races deep when a tax discussion makes them realize school funding actually matters more than they said. That has to be a first‑class action. Two ways in — a deliberate Edit themes link in the left rail, and a conversational catch when the user mentions something new in chat. Both land on the same compact amend editor, which appears inline in the chat thread so the journey stays on one page. After locking, the AI quietly re‑scores past decisions and only surfaces races where the new ranking would actually change the picture.

13 State party gates

What you're allowed to vote on depends on where you live.

Texas runoffs lock you to the party you voted in March. Pennsylvania closes its primaries to registered party members only. California puts every candidate on one ballot regardless of party. Until we know which ballot to use, the chat literally cannot run safely — it would research the wrong races. A small, state‑aware gate appears between address entry and the cold open, only when the rules require it. For a Nov general election it doesn't appear at all.

Voter Choice · AI

I've got your ballot for Harris County, TX. Before we research candidates, I need one quick answer — Texas law gates the runoff ballot to the party you voted in March.

This is the only question we have to ask up front. It changes which races you'll actually see on May 27.

Why this gate exists

Asking once, well, beats asking poorly five times.

If the chat doesn't know which party ballot to research, it'll waste turns researching races the user isn't allowed to vote in. The gate is upstream of the AI — by the time the chat starts, "DEM runoff" is part of the system context and there's nothing to clarify.

Cite the statute. "Texas Election Code §172.087" reads like authority, not surveillance. The user is being told a rule, not interrogated. Drops resistance to answering.
"I'm not sure" is a real answer. Voters who don't remember whether they voted in March shouldn't be locked out — the AI can walk through identifying questions. The gate respects ambiguity.
Skips for general elections entirely. No gate shown for Nov 3 — every voter gets one ballot, no party choice. The gate is data‑driven; if the state×electionType combo has no rule, the screen never appears.
The selection becomes AI context. Internally: {state: "TX", county: "Harris", ballot: "DEM-runoff", electionDate: "2026-05-27"}. The chat downstream knows what universe of races to talk about.

State rule is data, not code.

Each entry is a JSON record. Adding a new state means adding a row, not writing logic. The view always renders the same component shape — radio list + statute box — populated from data.

A · 20
Closed states need a graceful "you can't" path.

In PA, NY, and similar states, an unaffiliated voter literally cannot vote in a primary. The gate isn't a question for them — it's a clear "not eligible, here's how to register before next cycle." Trying to hide that would erode trust.

A · 21
The gate is upstream of every AI call.

Selection becomes part of the system prompt's <ballot_context> block. The chat never has to ask "which party are you voting?" mid‑conversation. It already knows.

14 Rewriting the chat prompt

From a 12K‑character mega‑prompt to a fleet of small task prompts.

The current ballot prompt tries to be a complete app inside a chat window — gather the user's location, walk them through issues, research candidates, generate outputs, handle the handoff. Our UX does most of that with UI. The chat's actual job is much smaller: extract themes from natural language, answer specific questions about a candidate or race, generate the handoff block when asked. Each of those is its own prompt — short, focused, easier for Haiku to follow.

Too big

12,000 characters is a context window crime, especially for Haiku.

Haiku's instruction‑following degrades under big system prompts — it pattern‑matches the most recent rule and drops the others. The prompt currently has eleven "rules," seven steps, three output templates, and six formatting clauses. Pick two and Haiku will follow them; the rest are noise.

Fix: ~300 tokens per task, max. One job per prompt. The app picks which prompt to use based on what the user just did.
Wrong job

The chat is told to do work the UI already does.

"Walk me through the issues one at a time," "give me a clean printable summary," "ask 3‑4 quick questions about how I think" — these are screens, not chat turns. When the UI renders an issue picker, a printable ballot, and a priorities ranker, the chat shouldn't also be doing them with text.

Fix: chat does what only chat can do — accept open‑ended language and respond conversationally. Structured input/output happens in UI.
Contradictory rules

"4–6 bullets" vs "plain language" vs "3–4 sentences" vs "bold the takeaway."

The current prompt has six format rules that fight each other. Haiku resolves the conflict by ignoring all of them and writing whatever feels reasonable — which usually means a long bulleted response with bolded fragments.

Fix: one format rule per prompt, stated as a single sentence. "2–4 sentences default. No lists." Done.
Standalone‑bot assumptions

The prompt assumes the user pastes it into Claude and types their zip.

That's a fine fallback (and it's literally the handoff for out‑of‑budget). But it shouldn't be the main prompt for the live app. Inside the app, the user has already given the address, picked the ballot, locked in themes — the prompt shouldn't re‑ask.

Fix: two prompt families. (1) In‑app prompts — short, assume context exists. (2) Handoff prompt — the one the user pastes elsewhere. Don't conflate them.

A shared safety header on every call.

One short block, prepended to every system prompt regardless of task. Centralizes the things that absolutely cannot drift — non‑partisanship, no PII storage, source citation. Easier to audit when it lives in one place.

shared · safety header~90 tokens
You are nonpartisan civic research. Three rules that always apply: 1. Never recommend a candidate or party unless the user explicitly asks. Surface evidence, not verdicts. 2. Never invent votes, donations, endorsements, or quotes. If you don't know, name one public source the user can check. 3. Don't echo back the user's full name, address, DOB, phone, or ID even if they paste one. Use only city + state.
A · 22
One job per prompt, picked by view.

Cold open → theme‑extraction prompt. Workspace → race deep‑dive. Handoff button → handoff prompt. The app already knows the view; it just picks the right system prompt. Haiku gets a tight instruction set every time, never the full superset.

A · 23
JSON for structure, conversation for chat.

Theme extraction returns JSON because the UI renders the editable cards. Race deep‑dive returns prose because the user is reading it. Don't force structure where it isn't useful — and don't ask for prose when you need fields.

A · 24
Dynamic context lives in tags, not the prompt body.

<priorities>, <candidates>, <ballot_context>. Easy to inject server‑side, easy to log, easy to verify nothing PII slipped in. The prompt text itself stays static.

15 What I'd build first

If I were sequencing this for the November deadline.

  1. Workspace split + ballot pane. Biggest UX gain per hour of work. Even with the old chat behind it, just showing the ballot filling in transforms how the tool feels. Two days.
  2. The cold open as a free‑form prompt. Open question, user describes in their own words, AI mirrors back inferred themes with the user's exact phrases as evidence. Reorder/confirm before any downstream candidate scoring. No pre‑built issue list, ever.
  3. Civic‑API fallback with PII warning. Ship before any marketing — the moment a user can't find their local races without this, they bounce.
  4. State party gates + rules table. Three states first: TX (runoff overlay), PA (closed primary), CA (top‑two). Adding new states is just adding rows. Without this, primaries either research the wrong races or fail safety review.
  5. Refactor the chat prompt. Split BALLOT_PROMPT.md into the 5–6 task prompts. Keep the original as the out‑of‑budget handoff prompt. Haiku reliability goes up significantly with prompts under 1.5K chars.
  6. Candidate card visualization, text‑first. Build the data layout first — name, alignment rows, donor list — so the chart code is decoration, not load‑bearing. Solves the "viz broke and the screen collapsed" problem.
  7. Printable PDF. Lock the page to one Letter sheet. Add the user's own notes per race. Polling header at top. This is the only artifact that goes home with the voter — it has to feel real.
  8. Out‑of‑budget handoff state. Treat it as continuity, not a wall. "Use your own API key" is part of v1, not v2 — power users will hit the limit first.
  9. Homepage rewrite. Hero + address input + two real numbers. Logistics demoted to ballot‑pane footer.
  10. Mid‑session theme amendment + silent re‑scoring. The "themes are a living document" promise only works if changing them is friction‑free and doesn't punish the user with re‑interrogation. Both entries (rail link + chat catch) lead to the same inline editor.
  11. Polis neighbors view — bridges first, compass second. The "you're not alone" bars and bridge statements can ship with zero ML. The named‑cluster compass needs enough sessions to cluster meaningfully (~150 in‑county) so it can come later.
  12. Profile + handoff exports. Promote them out of the button row. Both as standalone cards on the workspace footer with clear preview of what's in the file.
